01 / Species overview

A seal found nowhere else.

The Hawaiian monk seal is one of the world’s most endangered seal species. It is endemic to the Hawaiian archipelago, meaning its natural range is centered here and it does not occur naturally anywhere else in the world.

Hawaiian monk seals are mostly solitary animals. They do not form large colonies, although several seals may rest near one another in loose groups on beaches.

Their range stretches from the main Hawaiian Islands northwest through Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument. Rare sightings have also been recorded at Johnston Atoll. NOAA estimates about 1,600 seals remain: approximately 1,200 in Papahānaumokuākea and 400 in the main Hawaiian Islands.

The population has been increasing gradually since 2013, but it remains small and conservation work is still important.

Population note: The figure is an estimate, not an exact count. NOAA’s species profile reports approximately 1,600 individuals.

02 / Habitat & environment

Life between reef and shore.

Monk seals use both marine and terrestrial habitats. They forage offshore and around reefs, then return to land to rest, molt, give birth, and nurse their pups.

⌁

Abiotic factors

Warm subtropical ocean conditions, sunlight, tides, waves, water depth, and beach shape all influence where seals can feed and safely haul out. Protected, shallow-water beaches are especially useful for pupping.

✿

Biotic factors

Coral-reef and seafloor communities provide fish, eels, octopuses, and crustaceans. Seals are part of this food web as predators, while also sharing the ecosystem with sharks and other large predatory fish.

⌂

Resting & pupping sites

Seals haul out on sandy beaches, coral rubble, tidepools, and volcanic-rock shorelines. They often choose quieter areas away from heavy surf and disturbance.

Two-thirds of their time is spent at sea.

Although beaches are essential resting and nursing places, Hawaiian monk seals spend much of their lives swimming and searching for prey in shallow and deep marine habitats.

03 / Anatomy & adaptations

Built for an underwater life.

A streamlined body, sensitive senses, and specialized diving responses help monk seals find food and move through the ocean.

Structural adaptation

Streamlined body & flippers

A smooth, tapered body reduces drag in the water. The hind flippers provide much of the propulsion while swimming, and the foreflippers help with steering and maneuvering.

Sensory adaptation

Eyes, whiskers & closed nostrils

Seal eyes are adapted for underwater vision and low-light conditions. Sensitive whiskers, called vibrissae, can detect small water movements that help seals track nearby prey. Their nostrils close while submerged.

Physiological adaptation

The diving response

Hawaiian monk seals can hold their breath for up to about 20 minutes and dive deeper than 1,800 feet. Like other diving mammals, seals can slow their heart rate during dives (bradycardia), helping conserve stored oxygen.

Foraging behavior

Searching beneath the surface

Monk seals search near the seafloor and often prefer prey hidden in sand or beneath rocks. They have been observed flipping rocks with their noses to uncover concealed animals.

Scientific detail: The dive-response explanation describes a well-documented adaptation in diving mammals. NOAA documents Hawaiian monk seal dives exceeding 1,800 feet; the exact heart-rate response can vary with activity and dive conditions.

04 / Life cycle & reproduction

A long start for a young seal.

A mother invests several weeks caring for a single pup. After weaning, the young seal must learn to find food and survive independently.

~11 months

Gestation lasts about eleven months. Females generally give birth on land to one pup.

5–7 weeks

The pup nurses while the mother stays nearby and fasts rather than leaving to forage.

Independent

After weaning, the pup begins learning to forage. Juveniles face a challenging early period.

Adulthood

Females commonly begin reproducing at 5–6 years in the main islands and 7–10 in the Northwest.

The nursing period is a major commitment.

Mothers typically remain with their pups for 5–7 weeks and do not leave to feed during this period. They use stored energy to produce rich milk. Hawaiian monk seals can live for more than 30 years, although few individuals reach that age.

05 / Ecosystem & food web

A hunter of the seafloor.

Hawaiian monk seals are generalist, benthic predators. “Benthic” means that much of their foraging happens at or near the bottom of the ocean.

Reef & seafloor prey

Reef fish · eels · octopuses · squid · crabs · shrimp · lobsters · sea cucumbers

→

Hawaiian monk seal

A flexible predator that searches for prey in sand, under rocks, and around reefs.

◉

Natural predators

Sharks can prey on pups. NOAA has documented Galápagos shark predation on pre-weaned and recently weaned pups at Lalo (French Frigate Shoals), a geographically specific threat.

⇄

Competition

In parts of Papahānaumokuākea, seals share limited prey with other large predators, including sharks and large jacks such as ulua.

⌖

Ecological role

By feeding on a wide range of bottom-dwelling animals, monk seals are one part of the reef food web and reflect the health of the marine ecosystem.

06 / Threats to survival

Small population. Many pressures.

Threats differ across the species’ range. Some are especially important in the main Hawaiian Islands, while others have had major effects on pups and juveniles in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands.

THREAT 01

Toxoplasmosis

The parasite Toxoplasma gondii reproduces in cats. Its eggs can leave through cat feces, enter streams and watersheds, and eventually reach coastal waters where monk seals may be exposed. Feral and free-roaming cats contribute to this pathway.

THREAT 02

Fishing gear & marine debris

Lost or discarded nets, lines, hooks, and other debris can entangle seals or cause injuries. Interactions with active fishing gear also occur. Removing abandoned gear reduces this risk, but debris can arrive from outside a local area.

THREAT 03

Human disturbance

Harassment, crowding, feeding, intentional harm, and other disturbances can affect seals, especially mothers and pups. Giving hauled-out seals plenty of space helps protect both wildlife and people.

THREAT 04

Food limitation & predation

Juveniles can struggle when prey is limited. In certain Northwestern Hawaiian Islands locations, sharks have been a significant source of pup injury and mortality. These pressures are not identical at every island.

THREAT 05

Habitat loss

Low-lying atolls are vulnerable to beach erosion and sea-level rise. Losing resting and pupping beaches can reduce the safe places seals need on land.

THREAT 06

Other health & natural risks

Other diseases, injuries, aggressive interactions between seals, and difficult conditions during early life can affect survival. Conservation teams monitor and respond to injured, sick, and entangled animals.

07 / Conservation proposal

Seals Paradise

A proposed coastal refuge along the southern shores of Maui. The concept is to protect quiet sandy beaches and nearby feeding habitat, while reducing the chance that fishing equipment and human activity harm Hawaiian monk seals.

01 · Protected beach areas

Designate suitable sandy shoreline for undisturbed resting and pupping, with clear viewing boundaries and seasonal protections when needed.

02 · Marine protection zone

Evaluate a nearby zone restricting commercial fishing and net use, designed around seal movement, reef habitat, and local community needs.

03 · Ghost-gear prevention

Pair gear restrictions with regular debris surveys, removal of abandoned nets, and reporting systems. A local closure alone cannot stop debris that drifts in from elsewhere.

04 · Healthy prey habitat

Protect reef and seafloor communities, monitor prey availability, and track whether conservation measures are improving habitat conditions.

05 · Monitoring & research

Work with wildlife scientists and response groups to record seal sightings, pupping activity, entanglements, and habitat changes over time.

06 · Community partnership

Involve Native Hawaiian communities, local fishers, residents, scientists, and resource managers in planning, decision-making, and ongoing evaluation.

Project concept, not an existing reserve: “Seals Paradise” is a student conservation proposal. A real sanctuary or fishing closure would require ecological assessment, consultation with Native Hawaiian communities and local fishers, and approval from the relevant state and federal authorities.
